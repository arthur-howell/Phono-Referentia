# PHONO REFERENTIA

### *Cartridge Testing for Phono Education*

## Reference Disc I — Dynamics & Vocals
**2026**

Phono Referentia started because I wanted better reference material for testing phono cartridges.

Most cartridge reviews use commercially released records. That's useful for telling people how a cartridge sounds with familiar music, but it creates problems if you want to record those tests, preserve them, and make the recordings available for other people to hear. I also wanted material written specifically to challenge different parts of a cartridge instead of hoping that a particular commercial recording happened to contain what I needed.

So I made my own record.

Reference Disc I is the first result of that idea.

---

## What is Phono Referentia?

Phono Referentia is the reference system I'm building for cartridge testing on my YouTube channel.

The channel is a **for-profit project**. Videos may be monetized, and the recordings, measurements, photographs, observations, and other material collected through Phono Referentia may be used in those videos.

I wanted that stated plainly from the beginning.

At the same time, I don't want the useful material generated by the project to exist only inside YouTube videos. The original masters, documentation, cartridge recordings, and other data will be archived whenever practical so that people can download them and make their own comparisons.

The basic idea is:

> **Make the music → make the record → play the same record with every cartridge → record the results.**

That gives me something I haven't had when reviewing cartridges: a source I know from beginning to end.

# Reference Disc I: Dynamics & Vocals

The first record concentrates on **dynamics and vocals**.

I didn't want a traditional test record filled with sweeps, tones, and tracking bands. Those have their place, but that's not what I'm trying to build here.

I want to hear what cartridges do with **music**.

The songs were created to give me different voices, arrangements, dynamic ranges, percussion, frequency content, stereo information, and levels of complexity. Some sections are intentionally easy to reproduce. Others are there specifically to make the cartridge work.

The final record has two sides:

| | Length | Primary Focus |
|---|---:|---|
| **Side A** | **19:44** | Dynamics and vocal reproduction |
| **Side B** | **16:56** | Complexity, tracking and high-frequency performance |

**Total program time: 36:40**

The record will be manufactured as a **12-inch, 180-gram stereo record by American Vinyl Company**.

---

# The Music

All of the music on Reference Disc I was created specifically for this project using an AI music-generation system under my control.

I generated the material, selected the results I wanted to use, assembled the tracks, determined their order, and created the final Side A and Side B masters on my own equipment.

Those final WAV files are the starting point for everything that follows.

They are being publicly archived before the physical record is made so that the original source remains available alongside the eventual vinyl recordings.

---

# Side A

### Dynamics & Vocal Reference

**Running Time: 19:44**

1. **Before the Record Slips Away**
2. **Edge of the Page**
3. **The Eutopian Dream**
4. **The Eutopian Dream (Extend)**
5. **Virtus in Arduo**

Side A is primarily about voices and dynamics.

There are quiet vocals, stronger vocals, different vocal textures, changing arrangements, percussion, transient information, and sections that move from relatively sparse to much denser presentations.

I'm interested in how a cartridge handles those changes.

Does a close vocal retain its texture? What happens to small changes in volume? Do louder passages remain separated or start collapsing together? How clearly does the cartridge reproduce the attack and decay of percussion? Does a voice change character when the arrangement around it becomes more complicated?

Those are the kinds of comparisons Side A is intended to support.

# Side B

### Tracking & Complexity Reference

**Running Time: 16:56**

1. **Stand Your Ground**
2. **Velvet Nights — Lounge N House Remix**
3. **We Were Over in the Little**

Side B gets progressively harder.

It contains more complex arrangements, substantial high-frequency information, percussion, cymbals, layered vocals, electronic instrumentation, and sections where several things are happening at once.

The location of that material on the record matters too.

The final track was deliberately made more difficult as it approaches the inner portion of the side. I increased its level and pushed more of the demanding material toward the **left channel**, placing additional emphasis on the inner groove wall.

That gives me a musical inner-groove test rather than a test tone.

If a stylus begins having trouble with high-frequency information, sibilance, cymbals, dense passages, or the left groove wall as it approaches the label, I want this side to make that apparent.

# From Master to Record

The archived WAV files are my originals.

Those are the files that will be sent for manufacture of the physical Phono Referentia record.

After that, the record itself becomes the reference source for cartridge testing.

```text
SIDE A / SIDE B DIGITAL MASTER
              │
              ▼
         VINYL RECORD
              │
              ▼
       CARTRIDGE / STYLUS
              │
              ▼
          PHONO STAGE
              │
              ▼
             ADC
              │
              ▼
       DIGITAL RECORDING
```

I'm keeping the terminology for these files simple:

**MASTER**  
The original digital Side A or Side B file used to make the record.

**DISC**  
The physical Phono Referentia record manufactured from those files.

**CAPTURE**  
A recording of that physical disc being played with a particular cartridge and stylus.

Or, more simply:

> **MASTER → DISC → CAPTURE**

# Cartridge Captures

I intend to record the **entire record** with each cartridge I test whenever practical.

I'm not limiting the archive to the one or two excerpts that eventually appear in a video. If I record all 19:44 of Side A and all 16:56 of Side B, I'll have a complete record of how that cartridge reproduced Reference Disc I.

That gives me much more material to work with when I'm evaluating a cartridge and writing the eventual video.

It also means I can come back to those recordings later.

Something I don't notice during the original review may become interesting after testing five or ten more cartridges. Instead of relying on old listening notes or trying to recreate the original test, I'll still have the complete capture.

Over time, the archive should start looking something like this:

```text
                         ┌─ Cartridge A → Side A + Side B
                         │
MASTER → REFERENCE DISC ─┼─ Cartridge B → Side A + Side B
                         │
                         ├─ Cartridge C → Side A + Side B
                         │
                         └─ Cartridge D → Side A + Side B
```

Every cartridge is reading music that came from the same digital masters and, whenever possible, the same physical reference copy.

The original masters remain available too, giving me another point of comparison when something in a cartridge capture catches my attention.

# What I'll Document

A cartridge recording isn't particularly useful as reference material if nobody knows how it was made.

Each capture will therefore include as much of the relevant setup information as I can reasonably document.

That will generally include:

- Cartridge
- Stylus
- Stylus profile
- Approximate stylus hours
- Headshell
- Turntable
- Tonearm
- Alignment
- Tracking force
- Anti-skate
- Cartridge loading
- Phono stage
- ADC
- Recording resolution
- Capture date
- Relevant setup notes

If something in the signal chain changes, I'll document that too.

I'm not trying to claim that every variable in analog playback can be eliminated. That's obviously not possible.

What I can do is keep track of the variables well enough that I know what equipment and settings produced a particular recording.

# The YouTube Project

Phono Referentia exists because I'm building a **for-profit YouTube channel around phono cartridge testing and analog audio**.

The videos are the finished product.

Phono Referentia is part of the system I use to make them.

When I evaluate a cartridge, I can use the reference record to collect listening impressions, recordings, measurements, microscope images, setup information, and anything else that turns out to be useful. I can then use the complete cartridge captures while writing the script rather than relying entirely on what I remember hearing during one listening session.

A finished video may include things such as:

- Listening observations
- Recorded comparisons
- Measurements
- Cartridge specifications
- Stylus geometry
- Setup information
- Microscope images
- Tracking observations
- Comparisons with previously tested cartridges
- Excerpts from the Phono Referentia recordings

The channel may be monetized through YouTube and may eventually make money through sponsorships, affiliate relationships, or other commercial arrangements.

I'm not calling Phono Referentia a noncommercial project when the reason I'm building it is to support commercial content.

What I do want is for the reference side of the project to remain useful independently of the videos.

If I tell viewers that Cartridge A handled a particular passage differently from Cartridge B, I want to preserve the recordings behind that comparison. Where practical, those recordings can be made available so other people can listen, analyze them, or disagree with me.

The video contains my evaluation.

The archive contains the material I used to make it.

# This Is Only Disc I

I don't intend Phono Referentia to stop with Dynamics & Vocals.

The plan is to periodically create another reference record built around a different part of cartridge performance.

Reference Disc I is:

### **Dynamics & Vocals**

The next one might be:

### **Frequency Extension & Instrumentation**

After that it could be imaging, percussion, acoustic instruments, tracking, or something I haven't thought of yet.

I'm deliberately not planning ten records in advance.

My testing methods will change. My equipment will change. I'll learn things from using this record that will affect how I make the next one.

There will probably be things about Reference Disc I that I eventually wish I had done differently.

That's fine.

Part of what interests me about making this a continuing series is that the records themselves will preserve that development.

If I make one periodically for the next decade, I'll eventually have a physical collection showing how the testing changed from year to year.

More importantly, I'll have the data that goes with them.

The masters.

The physical records.

The cartridge captures.

The measurements.

The scripts.

The videos.

The manufacturing records.

And the notes I made along the way.

That's what I want **Phono Referentia** to become.
