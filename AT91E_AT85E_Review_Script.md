# Phono Referentia — AT91E / AT85E Review Script

## Technics SL-QD33 P-Mount Series

### Cartridge
Audio-Technica AT91E — P-Mount

### Stylus
Audio-Technica AT85E / AT85-family elliptical stylus

---

## OPEN

There is absolutely nothing exceptional about this cartridge.

And I don't mean that as an insult.

This is the Audio-Technica AT91E that came with my Technics SL-QD33, now fitted with an elliptical AT85-series stylus.

It isn't exotic.

It isn't expensive.

It isn't the cartridge I'd choose if I were trying to extract every last bit of information from a record.

But after spending some time with it, I've come to appreciate what this cartridge represents.

Because this thing is remarkably good at just being... fine.

It plays records.

It sounds good.

It behaves itself.

And, most importantly, it asks almost nothing from the person using it.

That makes it a perfect place to begin Phono Referentia.

---

# WHAT IS P-MOUNT?

Before talking about the cartridge, we need to talk about the strange little ecosystem it belongs to.

P-Mount — properly, T4P — appeared around the beginning of the 1980s.

And the idea behind it was actually pretty ambitious.

Someone buying a normal cartridge has to deal with cartridge mounting, alignment, overhang, tracking force, anti-skate, wiring and compatibility with the tonearm.

If you know what you're doing, none of that is particularly frightening.

But why should playing a record require knowing any of it?

That was the problem T4P tried to solve.

Instead of treating the cartridge and tonearm as two almost completely independent pieces of equipment, T4P standardized the interface between them.

A proper T4P cartridge was designed around a cartridge mass of about 6 grams and a nominal tracking force of 1.25 grams.

The stylus position and cartridge dimensions were standardized too.

There aren't four tiny headshell wires to connect.

There aren't two screws sliding around in slots while you stare at an alignment protractor.

You plug the cartridge into the arm.

You install the retaining screw.

And you play a record.

That's basically it.

---

# WHAT TECHNICS WAS TRYING TO DO

I think it's important not to confuse P-Mount with "cheap."

That's what it eventually became associated with, but that wasn't the entire idea.

Technics used T4P on machines like the SL-10, and there were genuinely sophisticated P-Mount cartridges — including moving-coil designs.

The goal wasn't simply to make the cheapest possible record player.

The goal was to make cartridge installation predictable.

If the cartridge manufacturer and turntable manufacturer both followed the same standard, the consumer didn't need to become a turntable technician every time they wanted a new cartridge.

That's a really compelling idea.

Especially when you think about what was happening to audio at the time.

The Compact Disc was coming.

You could put a CD into a machine, press a button and get consistent playback without knowing anything about alignment, tracking force or stylus geometry.

Against that, the traditional phono cartridge starts looking remarkably complicated.

T4P was an attempt to make the turntable behave a little more like a consumer appliance without completely abandoning replaceable cartridges.

And for a while, it worked.

P-Mount became common across a lot of consumer turntables during the 1980s and into the 1990s.

But it never replaced the half-inch cartridge mount.

---

# WHY IT DIDN'T TAKE OVER

And this is where P-Mount becomes interesting.

Because technically, the basic idea makes a lot of sense.

Standardize cartridge weight.

Standardize geometry.

Standardize tracking force.

Standardize the physical connector.

Make cartridges interchangeable.

Remove setup errors.

That's a pretty elegant system.

But audiophiles tend to like adjustment.

We like choosing the cartridge.

Then choosing the headshell.

Then aligning it.

Then changing the tracking force by a tenth of a gram because we're absolutely certain we heard something.

Then changing it back.

The flexibility of the half-inch mount is also what allowed cartridge design to keep spreading in every direction.

Different cartridge masses.

Different compliance.

Different tracking forces.

Different body shapes.

Different tonearms.

Different stylus profiles.

Moving magnet.

Moving iron.

Moving coil.

And increasingly exotic designs above all of them.

T4P's greatest strength was standardization.

That was also its limitation.

By the time P-Mount became strongly associated with inexpensive consumer turntables, much of the high-end market had simply continued down another road.

Today, the situation is almost reversed.

The half-inch cartridge is everywhere.

P-Mount is mostly something you encounter because you own a turntable that was designed for it decades ago.

---

# AND YET, YOU CAN STILL BUY ONE

Which brings us back to Audio-Technica.

Audio-Technica still lists P-Mount cartridges.

The current AT85EP is an elliptical moving-magnet P-Mount cartridge specified for a 1-to-1.5 gram tracking-force range, with 1.25 grams as the standard setting.

Its stylus is a bonded 0.3 by 0.7 mil elliptical.

And that is essentially the kind of configuration I'm testing here.

That matters because I'm not interested in reviewing this as some unobtainable museum piece.

This represents something you can still realistically do with a P-Mount turntable today.

You can own one of these old Technics tables, replace the cartridge or stylus, and continue using it without converting the arm, modifying the table, or hunting down some absurdly rare component.

---

# WHY THE ELLIPTICAL STYLUS?

The stylus choice is deliberate.

I don't want to start Phono Referentia with the most exotic stylus I can physically attach to the cartridge.

I want a sensible baseline.

A basic elliptical stylus is almost ideal for that.

It's a meaningful step beyond a simple conical profile, but it isn't trying to be a MicroLine, Shibata, fine-line or some other advanced contact geometry.

It should retrieve enough information to show us what the cartridge can do without turning this into an exercise in extracting absolutely everything buried in the groove.

More importantly, it suits the character of the entire P-Mount system.

Simple.

Predictable.

Reasonably forgiving.

And easy to live with.

---

# THE SL-QD33 AS THE FIRST TEST PLATFORM

That's also why the Technics SL-QD33 is the first Phono Referentia test platform.

It's quartz locked.

It's direct drive.

The cartridge geometry is essentially predetermined by the P-Mount system.

Tracking force is built around the T4P standard.

I'm not swapping headshells and wondering whether Cartridge A was aligned half a millimeter differently from Cartridge B.

For this first group of tests, I want to change the cartridge and stylus while disturbing as little else as possible.

Later, I'll absolutely move into half-inch cartridges.

That's where things get considerably more interesting.

But P-Mount gives me a wonderfully boring place to start.

And boring can be very useful when you're trying to establish a reference.

---

# HOW DOES IT SOUND?

So.

How does the AT91E with this elliptical stylus actually sound?

Fine.

That's still the word I keep coming back to.

It's fine.

Nothing immediately jumps out at me as exceptional.

I'm not hearing some enormous soundstage that makes me reconsider everything I know about vinyl.

I'm not getting extraordinary detail retrieval.

I'm not hearing bass that suddenly transforms the system.

The top end isn't doing anything magical.

Vocals aren't reaching out of the speakers and grabbing me.

It just produces good sound.

Reliably.

And I think there's a temptation in audio reviewing to treat that as failure.

I don't.

---

# THERE IS VALUE IN "FINE"

Imagine someone who owns records but doesn't necessarily know where all of them came from.

Some are new.

Some came from a record store.

Some came from a thrift shop.

Some belonged to their parents.

Some have probably been played on equipment that wasn't particularly kind to them.

They don't want to inspect every record under a microscope.

They don't want five headshells.

They don't want to check alignment every time they change something.

They want to put a record down and listen to it.

This cartridge makes an enormous amount of sense for that person.

An elliptical stylus gives them perfectly respectable playback.

The P-Mount system takes most of the setup questions away.

The tracking force is modest.

The whole thing is predictable.

There is very little ceremony involved.

Put the cartridge in.

Put the record on.

Listen to music.

There is something genuinely appealing about that.

---

# IS IT AN AUDIOPHILE CARTRIDGE?

That depends entirely on what you mean by audiophile.

If being an audiophile means chasing maximum resolution, unusual cartridge designs, advanced stylus profiles and every possible improvement in playback...

No.

This isn't where I'd go.

It isn't where I'm going.

There are cartridges I find substantially more interesting.

But suppose your definition of an audiophile is someone who cares about reproducing records well and wants a system that is consistent, uncomplicated and properly behaved.

Then I have no problem with someone choosing this.

In fact, I respect the choice.

You're trading some ultimate performance and flexibility for something that traditional cartridge systems are surprisingly bad at providing:

**consistency and simplicity.**

---

# RECORDS OF UNKNOWN ORIGIN

And there's one particular use where I think this kind of cartridge becomes even more attractive.

Records of unknown origin.

If I've just bought a used record and I don't know its history, I'm not necessarily interested in making my most expensive or exotic stylus the first thing that touches it.

This?

Sure.

It's a competent elliptical cartridge.

It tracks at a sensible force.

Replacement options still exist.

Setup is essentially predetermined.

And it sounds perfectly good.

That makes this kind of P-Mount system an excellent everyday playback platform.

Not because it does anything spectacular.

Because it doesn't need to.

---

# THE VERDICT

Would this be my primary cartridge?

No.

It's not really my kind of cartridge.

I want more information.

I want more resolution.

I want to experiment with stylus profiles.

I want to hear what happens when we start pushing the format harder.

That's where Phono Referentia is eventually going.

But I understand this cartridge now.

And more importantly, I understand why somebody would deliberately choose it.

The AT91E and this elliptical stylus combination gives me good sound, consistently, with almost no effort.

That's not exciting.

It's useful.

And sometimes useful is exactly what you need.

---

# CLOSING

So this is where Phono Referentia begins.

Not with an exotic moving-coil cartridge.

Not with a thousand-dollar stylus.

Not with the cartridge I expect to eventually declare my favorite.

It begins with a P-Mount Audio-Technica on an old Technics.

Because before I start finding out how good this can get, I need a baseline.

And this is a remarkably good baseline.

It works.

It sounds fine.

It asks almost nothing from you.

And now we have somewhere to go.
